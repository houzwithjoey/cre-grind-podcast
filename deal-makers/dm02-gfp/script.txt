# SEGMENT 1 — ORIGIN STORY
NORA: The story starts with a company called Newmark and Company, founded in Manhattan in 1929. In 1952, an employee named Aaron Gural and his partners bought it. Newmark was a management and brokerage firm, but Aaron Gural started buying buildings around Manhattan, beginning with 230 Fifth Avenue.

THEO: So the family business was both brokerage and ownership from the beginning.

NORA: Exactly, and that combination matters later. Aaron's son, Jeffrey Gural, didn't plan to join. After college he went west to work for the California highway department. When he came back to New York, he worked for a construction company, Morse Diesel, and then joined the family business in 1972.

THEO: And then there's Barry Gosin.

NORA: The famous partnership. Gosin was working at a small leasing firm and was poaching tenants from the Gural family's buildings. So Jeff Gural decided he'd rather partner with him than compete. In 1978, the two of them bought Newmark from Jeff's father. And over the next few decades, they did two things at once. Gural expanded the ownership side, buying and repositioning buildings across Manhattan, including the Flatiron Building and 520 Eighth Avenue. Gosin built the brokerage and advisory business.

THEO: And in their early years, Gural's specialty was conversions.

NORA: That's the irony of this whole story. His passion for construction led him to convert old warehouses on the West Side into office buildings. Seventy years later, his nephew is converting old office buildings into apartments. The family has been in the conversion business the whole time, just in opposite directions.

THEO: So how does Newmark the brokerage become separate from GFP the landlord?

NORA: In 2006, Newmark partnered with the London firm Knight Frank and became Newmark Knight Frank. In 2011, the brokerage was sold to BGC Partners, the Cantor Fitzgerald spin-off run by Howard Lutnick. But here's the key detail. Gural has said Newmark itself didn't own the buildings. They were owned by separate partnerships that he managed. So when the brokerage was sold, the family kept the real estate.

THEO: Two businesses that had always lived under one name.

NORA: Right. For a few years, the ownership side was called Newmark Holdings, which was confusing because the brokerage was also called Newmark. So in 2017, the family renamed it GFP Real Estate. GFP stands for Gural Family Properties. Newmark went public that same year, and today it's one of the biggest brokerages in the world, still run by Barry Gosin.

THEO: And they still work together.

NORA: Constantly. When you read about GFP's financings, Newmark brokers are often the ones arranging the loans. The 25 Water Street refinancing and the 40 Exchange Place construction loan were both arranged by Newmark's debt team. The two firms have shared a history, and in some years an office address, for decades.

THEO: Who runs GFP today?

NORA: Jeff Gural is chairman. He's in his eighties and still very involved. His son, Eric Gural, and his nephew, Brian Steinwurtzel, were co-chief executives for years, and the family has said it wants to keep the business family-run for at least two more generations. Gural also has a parallel passion: horse racing. The family runs the Meadowlands Racetrack in New Jersey, along with other racetracks, casinos and sportsbooks.

THEO: And Steinwurtzel is the one we'll hear about most today.

NORA: He's the development side. A great detail: he's said he didn't want to work in real estate at first, because his earliest memories of the family business were from the late 1980s and early 1990s, when the market crashed. During business school he worked as an asset manager for a Newmark building near Times Square. And on his graduation day, his uncle put him straight to work building a bowling alley in Harlem.

THEO: That's quite a first assignment.

NORA: And it set the tone. His early big project was the Film Center Building at 630 Ninth Avenue, a landmark that involved structural work, rezoning and redesign. Then progressively bigger and more complex projects, until he was running all of GFP's development.
# SEGMENT 2 — WHAT THEY ACTUALLY DO
[MUSIC]
THEO: Segment two. What does GFP actually do? You said it's now two companies.

NORA: As of April 2026, yes. The first is GFP Real Estate, the legacy business. It's a vertically integrated owner, operator and property manager. It describes itself as committed to New York since 1952, and it handles acquisitions, asset management, leasing and property management in-house. Recent profiles describe it as owning and managing somewhere between seventeen and nineteen million square feet across seventy-plus buildings, depending on how you count.

THEO: And who are its tenants?

NORA: This is what makes GFP Real Estate unusual. It isn't chasing big corporate headquarters. It focuses on small and medium-sized tenants and nonprofits. It serves more than twenty-five hundred tenants, and about a third of them are not-for-profit organizations. Steinwurtzel has described it as the largest landlord of small and medium-sized tenants in New York City.

THEO: Why would a landlord choose small tenants over big ones?

NORA: Diversification and staying power. If one big tenant leaves, a building can go from full to half-empty overnight, like what happened to the Flatiron Building, which we'll get to. With hundreds of small tenants, any single departure is a rounding error. The legacy portfolio is mostly what the industry calls Class B and C office, older buildings in neighborhoods like SoHo, NoHo and the Garment District. Gural has said his goal for those buildings is simple: keep them cash-flowing so he can keep making distributions to the family.

THEO: So the legacy business is the family's cash machine.

NORA: That's how they describe it, the breadbasket of the family business. And it does use leverage. GFP regularly refinances those buildings with bank loans. In early 2026, for example, it closed an eighty-six and a half million dollar refinancing from Apple Bank on 515 Madison Avenue, a forty-two story building in the Plaza District, and used the deal to buy out a minority partner, taking its ownership to one hundred percent. It also took a ninety-five million dollar bridge loan from BNY Mellon on a group of NoHo office buildings.

THEO: Though it hasn't all been smooth.

NORA: No, and it's worth being honest about that. In 2023, the same 515 Madison Avenue, GFP's home base at the time, defaulted on a one hundred three million dollar loan, and GFP negotiated a three-year extension instead of facing foreclosure. Even a well-run family landlord with high occupancy got squeezed when office values and rates moved. Note that by 2026, they'd refinanced it.

THEO: Okay, and the second company?

NORA: GFP Development, launched in April 2026, with Brian Steinwurtzel as chief executive. It formalizes the development and construction platform that had been growing inside GFP for years: conversions, ground-up development and big repositionings in New York City and Jersey City.

THEO: Why split it off?

NORA: Jeff Gural's explanation was refreshingly candid. He said every time Brian did a deal, the press would write "Jeff Gural's GFP is doing something," with his picture. He said that was silly, and he wanted a separate company where people know it's Brian's, and where the people working with him can have a piece of the action.

THEO: That last part is important.

NORA: Very. In development, talent is often paid with a share of the profits on deals, called promote or carried interest. A separate entity makes it much easier to give the development team ownership in what they build. Eric Gural continues as principal of GFP Real Estate, and Jeff Gural continues as chairman and chief executive of that side.

THEO: And how does GFP Development fund its deals?

NORA: Mostly with partners. The firm has said that while one early project is on land the family has owned for decades in Jersey City, most GFP Development projects will be newly acquired and financed by large private equity funds, the family's own equity and other sources. So GFP Development behaves a lot like the Related model we talked about last episode, at a smaller scale: the developer brings expertise and some equity, and big institutional partners bring most of the capital.

THEO: Jersey City. That's interesting.

NORA: The family's history there goes back to the 1980s, when Jeff Gural bought 150 Bay Street, a 1908 landmarked warehouse that had been converted into residential lofts, artist space, retail and some offices. GFP also owns ground-leased land on 15th Street and Coles Street in Jersey City, which it refinanced in early 2026 with a twenty-five and a half million dollar loan from Apple Bank. That's land where the family collects ground rent from others' buildings.

THEO: So how does GFP make money, across both companies?

NORA: On the legacy side: rent, minus operating costs and debt service, distributed to the family over decades. On the development side: buying obsolete buildings cheaply, converting them, and creating value, which shows up in a refinancing, a sale, or long-term rental income. Plus development fees and promote from partners. And unlike a fund, the family doesn't need to sell on a fixed schedule. That patience is a real advantage.
# SEGMENT 3 — THE DEALS
[MUSIC]
THEO: Segment three, the deals. Start with the one that made GFP famous: 25 Water Street.

NORA: 25 Water Street is a building in the Financial District completed in the late 1960s. It was a giant office and storage tower whose facade was designed to look like an IBM punch card, which is not exactly a compliment. Over the years it housed JPMorgan Chase operations and newspapers including the New York Daily News. About one point one million square feet.

THEO: Not exactly a natural apartment building.

NORA: Not at all. Huge, deep floor plates, which is the classic problem with conversions: apartments need windows, and the middle of a big office floor is far from any window. In 2022, GFP teamed up with Metro Loft Management, run by Nathan Berman, probably the most experienced conversion developer in New York, and with Rockwood Capital as the financial partner. They bought the building for two hundred fifty-one million dollars. Steinwurtzel has said that at the time, most people thought they were crazy.

THEO: And then the financing.

NORA: A five hundred thirty-six million dollar construction loan from MSD Capital, which is Michael Dell's family office investment firm, and Apollo. They re-clad the building with new, larger windows, added floors on top, and designed it with around fifty apartments per floor. The architect was CetraRuddy, which became GFP's go-to conversion architect.

THEO: The result?

NORA: SoMA, short for South Manhattan. One thousand three hundred twenty apartments and about a hundred thousand square feet of amenities. Leasing began in January 2025, with studios starting around thirty-six hundred dollars a month and three-bedrooms around ten thousand. It became the largest office-to-residential conversion in the United States.

THEO: And there's a tax program story here.

NORA: The key one. SoMA was the first project to use a New York State tax incentive called 467-m, which the state passed in 2024 specifically for conversions. GFP advocated for it. In exchange for a big property tax break, the owners set aside a quarter of the apartments, three hundred thirty units, as permanently affordable for households earning between forty and ninety percent of the area median income. In return, the building gets a ninety percent property tax exemption for thirty-five years.

THEO: Thirty-five years of ninety percent off property taxes is enormous in Manhattan.

NORA: It's the difference between a project working and not working. Steinwurtzel has said that without the incentive, all the apartments would have been market rate, and the program is structured on a sliding schedule: projects that started by June 30, 2026 get thirty-five years, by June 2028 get thirty, and by June 2031 get twenty-five. It also has no construction wage requirements, unlike the similar program for new construction. So there's a clock running on the deals that get the best terms.

THEO: And the payoff?

NORA: In December 2025, the partners refinanced SoMA with an eight hundred thirty-five million dollar debt package from Apollo and GIC, Singapore's sovereign wealth fund. Compare that to the two hundred fifty-one million purchase price and the five hundred thirty-six million construction loan. We'll do the math in a minute.

THEO: Deal number two. 222 Broadway, now Wrey.

NORA: This is the one from the cold open. A thirty-one story tower from 1961 across from City Hall Park, the former Western Electric Building, whose past tenants included Bank of America, Santander and American Express. Deutsche Bank's asset management arm paid five hundred two million for it in 2014. GFP and TPG Real Estate bought it in the spring of 2024 for one hundred forty-seven and a half million.

THEO: TPG, the private equity giant.

NORA: Exactly, a classic pairing of a big private equity partner with a local developer-operator. The partners secured about two hundred eighty-eight million dollars of construction financing from BDT and MSD Partners, again with Newmark arranging. They demolished a section of the upper floors, added a new steel-framed extension with a rooftop pool, and created seven hundred eighty-eight apartments, many with dedicated home-office nooks, plus a five-floor residents' club they call Two Two Two.

THEO: And the pricing?

NORA: Aimed at the top of the rental market. Studios with home offices start around forty-six hundred dollars a month, and three-bedrooms around twelve thousand. Steinwurtzel's thesis is that there's a lot of demand from professionals who want to live near Tribeca and can't find supply. At the time leasing launched, GFP said it was still deciding whether to opt into 467-m, which would mean designating a quarter of the units as affordable.

THEO: Deal number three. 40 Exchange Place.

NORA: A twenty-story building from 1893, on a site that once housed the New York Stock Exchange. GFP has actually owned it since 2015, when it bought it for one hundred fifteen million. In early 2026, GFP secured a one hundred ninety-one and a half million dollar construction loan from Derby Lane, a real estate credit firm, to convert its roughly three hundred thousand square feet into three hundred eighty-two apartments, a mix of market-rate and affordable, plus ground-floor retail. It's GFP's third residential conversion in Lower Manhattan.

THEO: Notice that one's a building they already owned. They didn't have to buy it cheap.

NORA: Right. That's a different situation: an older office building in the family portfolio that becomes worth more as housing than as offices. It's a preview of what could happen across more of the legacy portfolio over time.
THEO: Deal number four. The Flatiron Building.

NORA: The most famous building in this story, and the messiest backstory. The Flatiron, completed in 1902, was owned by a group that included GFP, the Italian firm Sorgente Group, ABS Real Estate Partners and an individual investor. When its single big tenant, Macmillan Publishers, left in 2019, the owners couldn't agree on how to pay for the renovation the building needed. That dispute ended in an auction of the building in 2023.

THEO: And the auction was a saga.

NORA: The first winning bidder couldn't come up with the money and defaulted. A second auction followed, and a GFP-led group won it with a bid of about one hundred sixty-one million dollars. At first the plan was to renovate it as offices. Then, later in 2023, the Brodsky Organization, a family-owned residential developer, bought a stake and took the lead on converting it into luxury condominiums, alongside Sorgente and GFP.

THEO: How many apartments?

NORA: Early filings talked about sixty units. The final plan became about forty, with most floors holding two residences, plus ground-floor retail. Brodsky has said selling all the units should bring in more than seven hundred million dollars. In April 2026, a full-floor penthouse asking fifty-nine million dollars went into contract. And in early November 2026, the Flatiron welcomed its first residents in its hundred-and-twenty-four-year history.

THEO: That's a good example of the single-tenant risk you mentioned earlier.

NORA: Exactly. One tenant leaves, the building goes from full to empty, the partners disagree, and the only way forward is a change of use. Note GFP's role here is a co-owner rather than the lead developer. Brodsky is managing the conversion.

THEO: Deal number five. 100 Gold Street. This one's different.

NORA: Very different, because the seller is the city. 100 Gold Street is a nine-or-ten-story, roughly six hundred fifty-six thousand square foot government office building from the 1960s, next to the Brooklyn Bridge ramps. It houses the city's Department of Housing Preservation and Development, which is the agency that runs affordable housing, along with other agencies and a senior center.

THEO: So the housing agency's own building is being turned into housing.

NORA: Which is a nice bit of symmetry. In March 2025, the city's Economic Development Corporation put out a request for proposals. In December 2025, it picked GFP. The original plan was about thirty-seven hundred apartments, with at least twenty-five percent permanently affordable. The money from selling the site goes toward new office space for the agencies moving out. Unlike the other projects, this one is a full replacement: the old building comes down, and a much bigger high-rise goes up, so it falls under the newer 485-x tax program for new construction, with its wage standards.

THEO: And it's grown since.

NORA: In September 2026, Mayor Zohran Mamdani's administration and GFP Development announced that the project would be expanded to nearly four thousand homes, with about one thousand permanently affordable, as it entered the city's public land use review. Steinwurtzel has said he sees 100 Gold as a template for how the city can award future conversions of public buildings.

THEO: Was there any controversy?

NORA: Some, and it's public. Reporting by the news outlet The City noted that GFP had donated forty-five thousand dollars to a super PAC supporting Mayor Adams's re-election bid while the bidding was open. The city said the selection was based on GFP's experience and commitments. Some housing advocates also argued the city should have used its land for deeper affordability. That's the kind of scrutiny that comes with public land deals, and worth knowing about.

THEO: And finally, the one that got away. The Chrysler Building.

NORA: In March 2026, The Real Deal reported that GFP was in advanced talks with Cooper Union, the college that owns the land under the Chrysler Building, to take over the building's ground lease. Coverage speculated that part of the tower could become residential, like the Flatiron. But in October 2026, Cooper Union finalized a hundred-and-fifty-year ground lease with Tishman Speyer and Canada's Public Sector Pension Investment Board, which plan to invest two hundred thirty-five million dollars and reposition it as a boutique office building.

THEO: So GFP doesn't win them all.

NORA: No developer does. But the fact that GFP was in advanced talks on the Chrysler Building tells you how far its reputation had come. A decade ago, a family Class B landlord wouldn't have been in the conversation for one of the most famous towers in the world.
# SEGMENT 4 — HOW CONVERSION MATH WORKS
[MUSIC]
THEO: You promised math. Let's talk about why conversions work, using GFP's own public numbers.

NORA: The whole game is basis, meaning what you pay for the building. Take 222 Broadway. One hundred forty-seven and a half million dollars for about seven hundred seventy thousand square feet is under two hundred dollars a square foot. Spread across seven hundred eighty-eight apartments, that's about one hundred eighty-seven thousand dollars of building per apartment. In Lower Manhattan, that's an extraordinarily low starting point.

THEO: And the construction?

NORA: The construction financing was about two hundred eighty-eight million dollars, which works out to roughly three hundred sixty-five thousand dollars of loan per apartment. So the lender alone is putting in about twice what the building itself cost. That tells you something important: in a conversion, the cost of the building is often the small number. The money goes into the work: new windows, new plumbing in every unit, new elevators and mechanical systems, extra floors, amenities.

THEO: Now SoMA, because there we have the refinancing.

NORA: Right. Purchase price: two hundred fifty-one million, about one hundred ninety thousand dollars per apartment. Construction loan: five hundred thirty-six million, about four hundred six thousand per apartment. Then, once the building was finished and leasing, the refinancing: eight hundred thirty-five million, about six hundred thirty-three thousand dollars per apartment.

THEO: So the new loan is roughly three hundred million dollars bigger than the construction loan.

NORA: That's the payoff. A lender was willing to lend about eight hundred thirty-five million against the finished, leased building, more than three times the purchase price. For a lender to do that at reasonable leverage, it has to believe the finished building is worth substantially more than that. That gap between total cost and finished value is the value the developers created. Refinancing lets them pull some of it out as cash, or at least lock it in, without selling.

THEO: And this connects to everything we've talked about on The CRE Grind.

NORA: Very much. These are the same ideas from the capital stack episode. A developer buys at a low basis, borrows against a business plan with a bridge or construction lender, executes, and then refinances into cheaper, longer-term debt once the asset is stable. And here's where the tax program comes in: 467-m's ninety percent property tax exemption directly raises the building's net operating income, and higher income supports a bigger loan and a higher value.

THEO: So what are the risks?

NORA: Four big ones. One: the building has to physically work. Deep floor plates, too few windows or the wrong column spacing can kill a conversion before it starts. Two: construction risk, with costs and delays on old buildings full of surprises. Three: leasing risk. You're adding hundreds of luxury units at once, and they need to rent at the prices the model assumed. And four: policy risk. The tax incentive has deadlines, and Steinwurtzel himself has said he expects the conversion pipeline to slow unless the city or state extends incentives, or the office market falls back into crisis.

# SEGMENT 7 — IF YOU'RE INTERVIEWING THERE
[MUSIC]
THEO: Final segment. Say you're interviewing at GFP Development or GFP Real Estate. What do you need to know?

NORA: First, know which company you're talking to, because they're different jobs. GFP Real Estate is about operating and leasing a big portfolio of older buildings full of small tenants: asset management, leasing, refinancing, tenant relationships. GFP Development is about acquisitions, entitlements, construction, financing and lease-up on large, complex projects. Mixing them up in an interview would be a bad sign.

THEO: Second?

NORA: Know the deals with numbers. SoMA: bought for two hundred fifty-one million in 2022, a five hundred thirty-six million construction loan from MSD and Apollo, thirteen hundred twenty units, the first 467-m project, and an eight hundred thirty-five million refinancing from Apollo and GIC. Wrey: bought for one hundred forty-seven and a half million with TPG, after Deutsche Bank paid five hundred two million in 2014, and seven hundred eighty-eight units. 40 Exchange Place: a one hundred ninety-one and a half million construction loan from Derby Lane. NORA: Understand 467-m, because it sits at the center of their recent strategy.
